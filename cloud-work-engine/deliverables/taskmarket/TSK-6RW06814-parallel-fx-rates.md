# Why Official and Parallel Exchange Rates Diverge, and How Economies Can Reconcile Them

*Taskmarket research draft — TSK-6RW06814*  
*Prepared: 11 November 2026 | Status: draft only, not submitted*

## Abstract

An official exchange rate and a parallel-market rate diverge when the price announced or administered by the state cannot clear the market for foreign currency. The gap is not simply a story of speculation: it often reflects a shortage of usable foreign exchange, rationed access, fiscal and monetary imbalances, restrictions on payments or capital movements, and a loss of confidence in domestic money. Unification can close the visible price gap, as Egypt did in March 2024 and Nigeria began doing in June 2023, but a single quoted rate is not the same as a functioning unified market. Durable convergence requires credible macroeconomic stabilisation, transparent and broadly accessible FX trading, adequate liquidity, and policies that restore confidence without imposing an abrupt and socially destabilising adjustment.

## Why two rates emerge

Foreign exchange is scarce whenever the demand for foreign currency at the official price exceeds the amount that exporters, investors, remitters, tourists and the central bank are willing or able to supply. If authorities hold the official rate above the currency's market-clearing value, they must either sell reserves, restrict access, or allow an alternative market to emerge. When reserves are inadequate or intervention is unsustainable, administrative allocation replaces price adjustment.

The resulting parallel rate is a scarcity price. Importers who cannot obtain dollars through official channels may pay more through intermediaries; households may seek foreign currency to protect savings; firms may need it to service external debt or pay suppliers. A persistent premium therefore communicates information that an administratively fixed official rate may conceal: the marginal cost of obtaining foreign currency and the market's expectations about future policy.

Several forces reinforce one another:

1. **Foreign-exchange rationing and import demand.** Import licences, priority lists, documentation rules, delayed bank allocations and outright prohibitions push legitimate transactions outside the official window. If imported inputs are essential and production cannot wait, businesses have an incentive to pay the parallel premium. Restrictions can also encourage under-invoicing exports, over-invoicing imports and other avoidance strategies.
2. **Inflation and fiscal dominance.** When large fiscal deficits are financed directly or indirectly by central-bank money creation, domestic prices rise and residents seek a store of value. If the official exchange rate is held fixed while domestic inflation continues, the currency becomes increasingly overvalued in real terms. The parallel market adjusts sooner than the official quotation.
3. **Capital controls and uncertainty.** Limits on transfers, repatriation, foreign-asset purchases or access to foreign currency can create an offshore or informal premium. When households and firms doubt that rules will remain stable, they may prefer foreign-currency assets even at a high price.
4. **Multiple official windows.** Preferential rates for fuel, food, government entities or selected firms create rents for those who receive cheap FX and costs for those excluded. A market with several administered prices is not economically unified merely because each price is officially published.
5. **Remittances, tourism and informal supply.** Migrants and visitors may route currency through informal channels if official conversion offers a substantially worse rate or is difficult to access. The parallel market can consequently attract supply as well as demand. The size and composition of these flows are often poorly measured.

The IMF's analysis of parallel markets describes how rationing, import restrictions, portfolio diversification and capital-flight motives interact. It also cautions that a one-off official devaluation does not, by itself, eliminate the long-run premium if the underlying restrictions and imbalances remain.

## What country experience shows

**Egypt, March 2024: a sharp reset supported by a broader package.** The Egyptian authorities moved to a more flexible exchange-rate regime on 6 March 2024. The official pound depreciated by about 38 percent that day, closing the large local parallel-market gap. The IMF reported that foreign-exchange backlogs at banks, previously estimated at US$7–8 billion, were cleared after unification. Its subsequent assessment used more than the headline rate as evidence: the spread with the local parallel market remained essentially closed, while daily interbank FX turnover rose to US$300–900 million from roughly US$20–40 million before unification. The episode illustrates why price adjustment, access to FX and actual market turnover must be assessed together. It does not prove that inflation or external vulnerabilities disappear immediately.

**Nigeria, June 2023 onward: unification compressed the premium, but adjustment remained costly.** Nigeria unified its FX windows in June 2023 and moved toward a floating arrangement in 2024. The IMF's 2026 analysis estimates that the parallel-market premium, which had averaged around 30 percent and exceeded 70 percent in acute stress episodes during 2016–17 and 2020–22, fell to an average of about 9 percent after unification. The exchange rate became more flexible and volatile, while monetary policy and market liquidity had to adjust. The lesson is not that floating automatically stabilises a currency; rather, a market-clearing rate makes scarcity more visible, and complementary monetary, fiscal and market reforms determine whether confidence can recover.

**Ethiopia: unification is not sufficient when market access remains constrained.** An IMF 2025 study found that a parallel premium persisted after Ethiopia's reform. It identified remaining current-account restrictions, a tightly closed capital account, low returns on domestic-currency assets and an underdeveloped FX market with limited competition and hedging instruments. The comparison with Angola, Egypt and Nigeria showed markedly different post-reform paths. This is a useful warning against treating a legal announcement of unification as proof that the market has become unified in practice.

## Can the two rates ever be reconciled?

Yes, but durable reconciliation is an institutional and macroeconomic process, not a single devaluation. A credible sequence generally includes:

- **A transparent, market-clearing price mechanism.** Banks and authorised intermediaries should be able to trade FX under clear rules, with access not restricted to politically favoured users. Auctions can help discover prices if participation is broad and allocation is based on bids rather than earmarked privileges.
- **A credible fiscal and monetary anchor.** Governments need a sustainable budget and a financing plan that does not rely on persistent monetary creation. Central-bank policy must contain inflation and communicate a consistent reaction function. Otherwise, a newly unified rate can quickly become overvalued again.
- **A workable transition for FX access.** Backlogs, priority lists and payment restrictions should be disclosed and resolved through a time-bound plan. Abruptly removing every control in a fragile economy can trigger capital flight; indefinite rationing, however, recreates the premium. Sequencing should protect essential imports and vulnerable households while progressively broadening access.
- **Liquidity and confidence.** Export receipts, remittances and legitimate investment should have incentives to return through formal channels. A usable interbank market, reliable settlement, hedging instruments and transparent reserve reporting help reduce the need for informal pricing.
- **Measurement beyond the headline rate.** Authorities should publish the official/interbank rate, transaction volumes, FX backlogs, reserve adequacy and a credible measure of the parallel premium. A narrow spread with negligible formal turnover is not meaningful unification.

## What the main policy choices can and cannot do

A **one-off devaluation** can close a quoted gap quickly, but without fiscal discipline and access to FX it may be followed by renewed depreciation and another premium. A **float** allows the exchange rate to absorb shocks and reduce the need for rationing, but it can transmit depreciation into import prices and inflation; monetary credibility and social protection matter. A **managed float** can smooth disorderly volatility, provided intervention does not defend an unsustainable level or recreate privileged allocation.

An **IMF-supported programme** can provide financing, a policy framework and external monitoring, but it is not a substitute for domestic implementation. Egypt's and Nigeria's experiences show that exchange-rate reform must be accompanied by credible monetary and fiscal measures, market functioning and the clearance of FX obligations. Programme financing cannot permanently defend an inconsistent price.

**Dollarization** or formal adoption of a foreign currency can remove the domestic currency's nominal exchange-rate risk, but it sacrifices monetary autonomy and seigniorage and does not solve fiscal insolvency, weak productivity or external competitiveness. It is a high-cost institutional choice, not a shortcut to credibility.

## Conclusion

Parallel exchange rates can converge, but only when the official market becomes a real market: foreign currency is accessible through transparent rules, the exchange rate can respond to scarcity, and fiscal and monetary policy stop generating persistent excess demand for FX. Egypt demonstrates that a large gap can close when price adjustment is paired with liquidity and policy changes. Nigeria shows that unification can sharply compress a premium while leaving a difficult stabilisation process. Ethiopia demonstrates that restrictions and shallow markets can preserve a premium even after formal reform. The durable test is therefore not whether two rates are announced as one, but whether firms and households can obtain and sell foreign currency legally, predictably and at a price that reflects genuine market conditions.
